stressed: /r/ as in "nurse", /er/ as in "square", /ir/ as in "near", /or/ as in "force", /)r/ as in "north", /ur/ as in "cure"
unstressed: /@r/ as in "letter"
While most mergers in American English are ongoing or marginall. English historically merge the /ɔr/-/or/ vowels (the NORTH-FORCE or HORSE-HOARSE merger), now, this distinction is primarily limited to African Americans. Merging /or/ with /ar/ instead (the START-NORTH or CARD-CORD merger) is limited to Utah, Texas and St.Louis, but is currently on decline in those areas as well.

/er/-ir/ mergers are limited to Carribeans and speakers (including whites) in Gullah country. In unmergerd dialects, words with /or/ include: horse, corn, cork, worsd with /our/ include: court, hoarse, born, store, adore

Non-rhoticity, or r-dropping, is recorded in Bermudians, African-Americans, and in whites in the coastal Northeast US and South since least the mid 1800s. Even rhotic Southern accents were ocassionally dropped r's word finally. Its connection to non-rhoticity in West African and British English is not directly attested. Non-rhoticity in Lunenberg, Nova Scotia, suggests a colonial New England presence as well.

The increasing prestige of rhotic American Englishes has lead to a rapid decline in non-rhoticity post-WWII in the US. For white Southerners, non-rhoticity, once associated with the wealthier Plantation Regions, being relatively uncommon in rural areas. It is now fully obsolete for younger white Southerners. For white Northeasterners it is increasingly a working class trait. It remains a stable feature of many African American Englishes.
