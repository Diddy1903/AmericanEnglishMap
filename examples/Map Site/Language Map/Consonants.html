Consonants
Consonants Apart from rhoticity, the most common consonantal variation associated with North America is, t flapping (or t tapping) and you-dropping. T flapping or pronouncing /t/ and /d/ as [r] when between two syllables. You-dropping, or reducing /y/ to ??? Stopping /th/ sounds is typical of non-natives learning English, and resultingly is a common feature of working class and minority accents throughout the continent. Pronouncing /th/ as [f] (th-fronting) is a notable feature of some regional forms of AAVE, it is characteristic of African Americans in the Northeast. Reducing /hw/ to /w/ in words like “what” “where” and “wheel” is a largely completed process in post-Independence US. The South, prior to WWII, was a rare holdout. Merging a reversal of /w/ and /v/, on the other hand is limited to 18th century colonies, and Anglo-Bahamians. Reducing /hy/ to /y/ is limited to NYC. Palatizing /k/ to /ky/ is recorded in the south. Palatizing /str/ to [štr] is a stereotype of Philadelphia. However there is evidence it is increasing among young people nationally. The opposite fronting /shr/ to [sr] in words like shrimp was historically seen in the South.
