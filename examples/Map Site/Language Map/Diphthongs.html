Diphthongs

tense: /oi/ as in "choice", /ai/ as in "price", /au/ as in "mouth", /ou/ as in "goat", /uu/ as in "goose" Apical consonants: /t/ /d/ /s/ /th/

Nucleus: The back upgliding vowels are the vowels ending with a [u] like quality. Increasing fronting of their nuclei after the 1800s is a primary divider of Northern versus Midland and Southern accents, however this sound change is often avoided in AAE. Fronting is also seen in Western states, as part of the California vowels shift.

In 18th century English /au/ had a central nucleus, retained mostly in Michigan’s Upper Peninsula, rural New England and apparently in 19th century Virginia, where /au/ was only lowered before /l/ and before or after /n/. In and near Canada, and historically in the Tidewater region and South Carolina, we instead see Canadian Raising, where /au/ is central before voiceless consonants, but low elsewhere.

While /ou/ is largely a “back upgliding vowel” today, it was a monophthong, [o:] in the 18th century. It remained so in 19th century AAE, and in dialects influenced by Spanish, Cajun French, German and Gullah. A subset of monophthongal accents also show downgliding [oa], like Gullah, Jamaican, and South Carolina.

The glide of /au/ is usually mid-height lowered in “Pittsburghese”, resulting in cliches like “dahntahn”. An unsolved mystery is why, like West Country English, pronouncing /au/ as [aü] (and /ai/ as [oi]), is found in High Tider, Lumbee and white Bahamian English: as most Loyalists sailing to the Bahamas departed from the North.
